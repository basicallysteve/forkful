Lesson 01

Expectation & Variance: from data you have to randomness you model

You already compute a mean and a variance from a column of numbers. This lesson upgrades those two familiar quantities into their probabilistic form — expectation and variance of a random variable — which is the language your ML course will actually speak. This one shift is the bridge from the descriptive statistics you know to the theory you said felt shaky.

Why this is lesson one. In machine learning, a loss function is an expectation (average loss over data you'll see), and a model's instability is a variance. Get these two right and the words "expected loss", "bias–variance", and "estimator" stop being jargon.

1. A random variable, in one breath

A random variable X is just a number produced by a random process — a dice roll, tomorrow's temperature, whether an email is spam (1) or not (0). We don't know the next value, but we know how the values are distributed. Everything below is a property of that distribution, not of any one sample.1

2. Expectation = the probability-weighted average

The expected value E[X] (also written μ) is what the average converges to if you could sample forever. For a discrete X:

E[X] = Σx  x · P(X = x)

Each outcome weighted by how often it happens. For a fair die that's 1·⅙ + 2·⅙ + … + 6·⅙ = 3.5 — note the "expected" value is a value the die never even shows.

Key idea — the bridge
The sample mean x̄ you already know is an estimate of E[X]. More data → the estimate homes in on the truth. That is the Law of Large Numbers, and it is why training on more data works. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Roll a fair die more and more times; watch x̄ approach E[X] = 3.5
for n in [10, 1_000, 1_000_000]:
    rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n)
    print(f"n={n:>9,}  sample mean = {rolls.mean():.4f}")

# n=       10  sample mean = 2.8000     ← noisy with little data
# n=    1,000  sample mean = 3.6020
# n=1,000,000  sample mean = 3.5022     ← converging on E[X]=3.5

The one property ML uses constantly: linearity

Expectation passes straight through sums and scaling:

E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + b     and     E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

The second holds even when X and Y are dependent — a small fact that quietly powers a lot of ML derivations.2

If E[X] = 5, what is E[2X + 3]?

3. Variance = expected squared distance from the mean

Variance measures spread as the average squared gap between X and its own mean:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]  =  E[X²] − (E[X])²

The right-hand form ("mean of the square minus square of the mean") is the one you'll actually compute. Standard deviation σ = √Var(X) puts the spread back into the variable's units. Two rules worth memorizing:

Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X)     Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)  (only if independent)

Scaling hits variance by a² (not a), and adding a constant b shifts data without changing spread.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.normal(loc=10, scale=2, size=100_000)   # true μ=10, σ=2, so Var=4

print(x.var())                       # 3.972  ← estimate of Var(X)=4
print((x**2).mean() - x.mean()**2)   # 3.972  ← same, via E[X²]-E[X]²
print((3*x).var(), 9*x.var())        # 35.8 35.8 ← Var(3X)=9·Var(X)
numpy footnote you'll want later. np.var divides by n. The unbiased sample variance divides by n−1 — pass ddof=1. Why the correction exists is a whole lesson (estimators & bias); for now just know the knob is there.

Your win

You can now read E[X] and Var(X) as properties of a distribution, connect them to the sample mean and variance you already compute, and use linearity and the scaling rules. That vocabulary is the spine of nearly every ML formula you'll meet next.
